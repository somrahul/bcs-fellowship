Section 3C · Additional sub-criterion — Invention and innovationStatement as submitted · 440 words
Situation

Commissioning a wireless lighting network used to mean walking the building. An installer had to physically reach every device to associate it, so a commercial floor took days. At Walmart the same shape arrived from a different direction: a photorealistic 3D asset needed an artist and cost hundreds of dollars per SKU. In ambient intelligence research, smart environments were still rule-based, with no blueprint for inferring intent or handling trust. Three problems, one shape. The expensive step was human, and everybody had decided that's just how the job works.

Task

The job was to take the human step out without giving up on correctness. You can't just make the installer faster. For commissioning that meant a method that could tell devices apart from radio signal alone. For 3D it meant a pipeline that could put a render-ready asset in the catalog without an artist. For ambient systems it meant writing the architecture down so others could build on it.

Action

Four inventions came out of that: RSSI proximity commissioning: I co-invented a method that sorts devices by RSSI gradient and filters them spatially, so a device identifies itself by where it is rather than by somebody standing next to it. That's the whole trick. Nine patents were granted and published across the US, Europe, China and Canada in three invention families, with US 11,438,225 B2, US 12,166,627 B2 and EP 3,935,791 B1 covering the commissioning method itself. It became the commissioning engine across Lutron Athena, HomeWorks, RadioRA 3 and Caseta, cutting installation labor 20% to 30%. It didn't stay inside Lutron: Acclivis Technologies licensed the architecture across commercial IoT controllers and Innovative Technology Solutions adopted it. The platform integrates with Samsung SmartThings. Apple and PassiveLogic cite it across more than ten patents. The generative 3D factory: At Walmart I architected the multi-stage asset engine behind the catalog, combining procedural geometry, neural texture prediction and diffusion models. Shop the Background came off the same platform, using Grounded DINO and SAM to decompose a photograph into shoppable items. Writing the architecture down: I wrote 'The Anticipatory Home: A Blueprint for AI, Spatial Computing, and Ambient Commerce', which introduces the Living Services Architecture. Professor Vaibhav Unhelkar at Rice University adopted it for graduate teaching in human-centered AI and autonomous agents.

Result

The commissioning work left the company, that's the test I care about most. Walmart's generative 3D engine took the catalog past 200,000 production models at 30% lower asset cost and half the turnaround. And a professor with no stake in my career put the Living Services Architecture in front of graduate students. That's a different kind of evidence than my own employer liking the work.
