Section 3B · Professional impact — Mentoring and coachingStatement as submitted · 421 words
Situation

Shipping AI to customers asks something of engineers and product managers that ordinary feature work doesn't. A model's output varies run to run. Latency budgets are real money. Privacy boundaries can't be bolted on at the end, and the failures that matter turn up in production rather than in a demo. Across Walmart, Lutron and two universities I kept seeing the same pattern. Capable people who could execute a well-specified component would stall the moment they had to own a system end to end. That isn't a skills gap. It's a judgment gap, and judgment doesn't transfer through documentation.

Task

I took direct responsibility for closing it. I coach product managers, software engineers, UX researchers and system designers across Walmart and Lutron, and I teach graduate students at the University of Pennsylvania and Lehigh. The goal is the same everywhere: get the person to where they make the architectural call themselves and can still defend it after it ships.

Action

The coaching happens in four places:

Making them argue it: I don't hand people answers. In design review I make a mentee argue for the alternative they rejected and name the failure mode they're most worried about. If they can't say what being wrong would cost, they aren't ready to sign the design off.

Ownership before launch: On the computer vision and AI audio work I won't approve a release until the runbook and the regression suite exist and somebody's name is on the observability dashboard. Operational ownership is a precondition, not a follow-up ticket.

Blameless post-mortems: After a production anomaly I coach the team to separate ordinary model variance from a real regression in the data pipeline. Those two get confused constantly, and the fixes are nothing alike. Guessing wrong costs a fortnight.

Teaching: At UPenn I built and delivered graduate masterclasses in Integrated Product Design on 'The AI Product Orchestrator' and 'The Evolution of Product Roles in the Age of AI.' At Lehigh I lectured on ambient computing and Zero UI architectures.

Result

Three of the people I've coached were promoted into Senior and Lead Product Manager and Engineering roles across Walmart and Lutron, and they now govern multi-million-dollar AI platforms rather than execute against someone else's spec. The change I look for is narrower than a promotion, though. People who used to come to me for a specification review now run their own architecture reviews and take the 2am production call without escalating it. Across UPenn, Lehigh and IEEE STEM outreach I've taught more than 100 graduate students and early-career engineers.
